A Little Bit of Myself

Where I come from, how I got here, what I care about, and what keeps me curious — a proper introduction for anyone landing on this site.

I've had a website for years but never written an actual "about me" post. The homepage has the résumé version — titles, companies, bullet points — but that's the skeleton, not the person. So here's a bit more of the flesh.

Where I'm from

My professional career started in Shanghai. I studied architecture and urban planning at Tongji University, which sounds like a strange path into software engineering, but it made perfect sense at the time. I was fascinated by cities as systems: how roads and zoning rules and population density interact to produce emergent behavior at scale. That instinct — thinking in systems — turned out to be the most transferable skill I ever developed.

My first job was at Tongji's urban planning design institute, writing software to help planners analyze and visualize the city. I was bridging AutoCAD, GIS data, and the web before "full-stack" was a word people used freely. I loved it. But I also started feeling the ceiling of the domain. I wanted to work on software itself — not just as a tool for something else.

How I got here

I left Shanghai in 2015 for Carnegie Mellon's Master of Software Engineering program in Pittsburgh. It was a deliberate reset. I wanted rigor — the kind that comes from being surrounded by people who've thought deeply about the same problems you're starting to ask questions about.

CMU delivered. The cloud computing course in particular — I was a TA for it — reshaped how I thought about distributed systems. Spark on EMR, Hadoop, cluster coordination problems: I got to both learn and teach, which is the best possible combination for actually internalizing something. Pittsburgh in the winter is a separate kind of character-building, but I'll leave that aside.

After graduation I joined Microsoft in Redmond, working on Azure Resource Manager — the control plane that sits beneath everything in Azure. For five and a half years I worked on the data layer: a storage service built on top of Cosmos DB, a large-scale zero-downtime migration, a CDC pipeline that started as a backup mechanism and ended up being used by multiple teams as a general-purpose inter-services communication bus. That last one still makes me smile. The best infrastructure disappears into the platform and gets used for things you never imagined.

In 2022 I joined Snowflake, where I've been working on Snowpipe — the continuous ingestion service. The scale is different here. The problems are different. But the core satisfaction is the same: making something that millions of data pipelines quietly depend on work a little better, a little more reliably, at a little lower cost.

What I care about in engineering

A few things I've come to believe pretty firmly after 15+ years:

  • Correctness is upstream of performance. If you don't know what the system is supposed to do, optimizing it is just making the wrong thing happen faster.
  • Availability is a product feature, not an engineering nicety. Downtime is something your users experience, not just a metric on a dashboard.
  • The best architectural decisions are the ones that age well. Not clever. Not novel. Just decisions that still look reasonable three years later when the people who made them have moved on.
  • Write things down. Not for documentation's sake — for thinking's sake. Writing a design doc forces you to confront the parts you've been avoiding.

I also think the industry undervalues people who can migrate things. Building new systems from scratch is glamorous. Migrating a live, production system that millions of customers depend on — with zero downtime, no data loss, no incidents — is genuinely hard and almost never gets a conference talk.

The side of me that builds for fun

I've always had side projects running in parallel with whatever I was doing professionally. Most of them live in the demos section of this site: a mind-mapping canvas, a speed-reading trainer, a guitar chord explorer, a bubble physics simulation. None of them are products. They're more like sketches — small proofs that a certain idea is possible, or that a certain interaction is satisfying, or just that I spent a Sunday afternoon chasing something interesting.

I play video games. Always have — it's how I decompress, and honestly how I first got interested in software as a kid. There's a certain overlap between the systems thinking I do at work and the way I approach games: understanding the rules deeply enough to find the interesting edges.

The thing I like most about engineering is that it rewards curiosity over cleverness. You don't have to be the smartest person in the room. You have to be the one who's most willing to go find out.

Why this site exists

Partly as a portfolio. Partly as a place to think out loud. Partly because I've been writing code since the mid-2000s and I wanted somewhere to put the things that don't fit neatly into a résumé or a GitHub repo.

If something here is interesting to you — a post, a demo, a career question, a distributed systems problem — feel free to reach out. My email is on the homepage. I'm usually slow to reply but I do reply.

Thanks for reading. More posts to come — no promises on cadence, but I have a few things I've been wanting to write about for a while.
